Free guide · Wills & estate basics

"Die without a will and the state takes everything" — the honest truth.

You have seen both reels: "Die without a will and the STATE takes everything — your kids get nothing!" on one side, and "Write your own will in 5 minutes — free, no lawyer ever, this one PDF protects your whole family" on the other. One sells panic, the other sells a shortcut. Here is the honest version: if you die without a will, your property generally passes to your closest family under your state's law — not to the state. A will is only one of three ways your assets move, beneficiary designations and how accounts are titled often matter more, and a do-it-yourself will only counts if it follows your state's signing rules. Plain English, free, with nothing to buy.

The reel's panic vs. what really happens

The reel's promise

  • "Die without a will and the STATE takes everything."
  • "Write your own will in 5 minutes — free, no lawyer, ever."
  • "This one PDF fully protects your whole family."
  • "Sign up today or your kids get nothing."
  • "Pay my fee and I'll 'protect your inheritance'."

The honest reality

  • Die without a will and your property generally passes to your closest family under state law. The state only takes it in the rare case no relative can be found. The real cost is delay, expense and losing your say, not "the state grabs it."
  • You often can write your own will — but only if it follows your state's signing and witness rules exactly. A form done wrong can be worthless when it counts.
  • No single PDF covers everything. Beneficiary designations and titling move retirement, insurance and many accounts — and they override the will.
  • Nobody legitimate pressures you to sign "now or your family loses everything." Manufactured urgency is the sales tactic.
  • Nobody legitimate demands an upfront fee to "protect your inheritance," and a stranger's DM about a will is not a will.

Almost never
is how often "the state takes everything" is actually true. In essentially every state, dying without a will means your property passes to your closest relatives by law — the state itself only inherits in the rare case no living relative at all can be found. The honest point is not that the state grabs your estate; it is that without a plan a court decides who gets what, and who raises your kids, instead of you.

What actually decides who gets what — and the will is only one part

Here is the piece both reels skip: your assets can move three different ways, and the will only controls one of them. Getting the other two wrong is the most common, quiet way a plan fails — all of this is general, and the exact rules vary by state.

1. The will itself

what a will really does

A will names an executor, can name a guardian for minor children, and directs assets that do not already pass another way. It is important — but it goes through the court (probate), and it does not override the next two. Think of it as the backstop, not the whole plan.

2. Beneficiary designations

retirement & life insurance

Retirement accounts and life insurance pass to whoever is named on them — and that naming beats your will. An ex-spouse still listed on a policy can inherit over the family in your will. Keeping these current often matters more than the will itself, and updating them is free with each institution.

3. How accounts are titled

joint & payable-on-death

A jointly owned home or bank account, or an account marked payable-on-death / transfer-on-death, usually passes automatically to the co-owner or named person, outside the will and outside probate. How something is titled can quietly decide who gets it — check each account.

4. The paper trail

organized records

None of the above helps if your family cannot find it. A plain, organized list — accounts, policies, documents, who to call — is what lets an executor act without a costly hunt. The through-line of every honest guide here: organized records are the paper trail that protects the people you leave behind.

Put simply: a will is one tool, not the whole plan. Beneficiary designations and titling often move the biggest assets and override the will, and organized records are what make any of it usable. "One 5-minute PDF protects everything" is the myth; the reality is a few coordinated pieces, most of which are free to review yourself.

The honest path — how estate basics really work

This is the real order of operations, in general terms. The exact rules, forms, witnessing requirements and procedures vary by state and county — find yours on your local court's or state bar's official self-help resources, and use a licensed estate attorney for anything beyond the basics. No "one-PDF-protects-everything" pack required; that is the trap.

  1. Step 0 · Know what a will can and cannot doA will is the backstop, not the whole plan. A will names your executor and a guardian for minor children, and directs assets that do not pass another way — but it does not control beneficiary-designated accounts or jointly titled property. Knowing that up front is what keeps a plan from quietly failing.
  2. Step 1 · Take inventoryList what you have and where it is. Write down your accounts, property, insurance, debts and key documents, and where each lives. This one plain list does more for your family than most "estate kits" — and it is free. It is also the foundation everything else builds on.
  3. Step 2 · Check your beneficiaries & titlingFix the parts that override the will — first. Confirm the named beneficiary on every retirement account and life-insurance policy, and how each account and property is titled. These often move the most money and beat the will, so they are usually the highest-leverage thing to get right. Updating a beneficiary is typically free with the institution.
  4. Step 3 · Get the will done properlyValid means it follows your state's rules. A will only works if it meets your state's signing and witness formalities, which vary by state. Use your state court's or bar association's official self-help resources, or a licensed estate attorney — especially if you have minor children, property, or anything complicated. A form that ignores your state's rules may not hold up.
  5. Step 4 · Store it safely — and tell someoneAn executor who can't find it can't use it. Keep the signed original somewhere safe but findable, and tell your executor and family where it is and how to reach your attorney. A perfect will nobody can locate is the same as no will. This is the paper trail doing its job.
  6. Step 5 · Keep it currentStale documents are a top failure mode. Review after any big life change — marriage, divorce, a birth or death, a major asset change, a move to another state. An out-of-date will or an old beneficiary can send your assets to exactly the wrong person. A quick periodic check keeps the plan honest.
What "the state takes everything" and "one 5-minute PDF" really are: fear and a shortcut — both sold to you. Dying without a will does not hand your estate to the state; it hands the decisions to a court, with more delay and cost. And a will is one part of a plan — beneficiary designations and titling move much of the money and override it. A do-it-yourself will can be fine if it follows your state's rules, but a form done wrong, or left stale, is where families actually get hurt. The honest fix is boring: know the pieces, keep them current, keep organized records.

Red flags — for the worried and for the targeted

Wills and estate planning are real and worth doing. The reels selling panic or a magic PDF and the scammers who fake inheritances are where people lose money. If you hear any of these — whether you are the one worried or the one being targeted — stop.

"Die without a will and the state grabs everything."Fear bait. Your property generally passes to your family under state law; the state only inherits when no relative can be found.
"Write your will in 5 minutes — guaranteed valid in every state."No form is valid everywhere. States set their own signing and witness rules; a will that ignores them may not hold up.
"This one PDF fully protects your family."Beneficiary designations and account titling often override a will. A single document rarely covers the whole picture.
"Sign today or your family loses everything."Manufactured urgency is a sales tactic, and sometimes a scam. Real planning is never a now-or-never button.
(Targeted) "Pay a fee now to 'release' or 'protect' your inheritance."A legitimate inheritance never requires an upfront fee, wire or gift card to a stranger. That is the inheritance scam.
(Targeted) "You've been named in a will — send a processing fee or your bank details."Real estates run through probate court with a case number and an executor. Verify with the court from its official .gov site; never send money or logins.
