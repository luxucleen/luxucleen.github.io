Where the real money actually is — four honest lanes

Estate cleanout is a genuinely good business. The income is real, steady and repeatable — it just comes from doing the work well, not from a jar of coins. Here is where the money honestly comes from.

1. Cleanout labor

charge for the work

The core service: clear the home, sort keep/donate/dispose, haul it out, and handle disposal. You charge by the job, the hour, or the truckload, plus dump fees. This is honest, needed work — families and executors will pay well for a crew that is insured, careful and on time.

2. Estate sale / liquidation

commission, for the estate

Instead of trashing everything, you can sell the contents for the estate and earn a commission on what sells — through an estate sale, auction or consignment. The proceeds belong to the estate; your written agreement spells out your percentage. This is the higher-value version of the job.

3. Referral partnerships

the repeat-work engine

Steady income comes from people who send you jobs: probate attorneys, realtors, senior-move managers and estate-sale companies. They refer families who need a home cleared. Do it clean and honest once, and the same partners feed you work for years.

4. Resale — done legally

buy it, then it's yours

You can resell estate items for a profit — the legal way is to buy them from the estate at a fair, documented price first. Once you have paid the estate and have a receipt, the item is yours to flip. The line is simple: buy it, don't take it.

Put simply: the money is in labor, liquidation, referrals and legal resale — all of it earned, all of it documented. "Keep what you find" is not a lane; it is the thing that ends the business and starts a police report.

The honest path — build a real cleanout business

This is the real order of operations to turn estate cleanout into a business families trust and partners refer to. No "keep the loot" shortcut required — that shortcut is the trap.

  1. Step 0 · Register the businessBe a real, findable company. Set up an LLC and EIN so the business is separate from you and looks legitimate to executors and attorneys. A registered entity is the first thing a probate attorney or realtor checks before they refer a grieving family to you.
  2. Step 1 · Insurance, bond & permitsProtect the family and yourself. Carry general liability insurance and get bonded — that is what lets a family trust strangers in a loved one's home. Follow local dump and transfer-station rules for disposal, check whether your area licenses junk-hauling, and add workers' comp if you hire. Rules vary by state and city; confirm on your .gov sites.
  3. Step 2 · The written contractEverything in writing, before you touch a thing. Sign a cleanout agreement with the executor or administrator that names: the scope, what gets hauled vs. returned, how valuables are handled (photographed and set aside for the executor), disposal, timeline and price. A written scope is what turns "you stole grandma's ring" into "here it is, signed for."
  4. Step 3 · Get the workBuild the referral engine. Introduce yourself to probate attorneys, realtors, senior-living communities, and estate-sale companies. List the business on Google with real photos and reviews. One clean, honest job for an attorney's client can become a steady pipeline — they need a crew they can vouch for.
  5. Step 4 · Do the job rightDocument, protect, return. On site, photograph before you start, sort carefully, and set aside anything of value — cash, jewelry, coins, documents, photos, guns — for the executor. Never assume something is trash. The crew that returns a found envelope of cash is the crew that gets every future job in that county.
  6. Step 5 · Grow itCrew, systems, reputation. Add a truck, a crew and workers' comp, standardize your contract and pricing, and stack reviews. Layer in estate-sale commissions for the higher-value homes. Honest reputation compounds — that is the real "$5,000 a house," earned the way that lasts.
What "keep what you find" really is: theft — and often a crime. Everything inside a deceased person's home is part of the estate and belongs to the heirs, distributed by the executor. Taking cash, jewelry, coins or heirlooms you "find" is stealing from a grieving family, and it can bring criminal charges and a lawsuit. A legitimate crew photographs and returns anything of value. If you did not buy it from the estate with a receipt, it is not yours.

Red flags — for the worker and the family

The business is real. The reels and the shady crews around it are where people get robbed or arrested. If you hear any of these — whether you are chasing the reel or hiring a crew — walk away.

"Keep everything valuable you find."Everything belongs to the estate. "Finders keepers" is theft, not a business model.
"No license or insurance needed."Uninsured crews leave the family liable for injuries and damage. Real cleanout businesses are insured and bonded.
"Cash only, no contract — just show up and haul."No written scope is how valuables "disappear" and how you get sued. Everything in writing, every time.
"Buy my $497 course to learn the secret."The real steps — register, insure, contract, refer — are free and on this page. The course is the actual product being sold.
(Family side) "We'll clear it for free — we just keep the contents."You may be handing away valuable items with no accounting. Get a written contract and an itemized plan for anything of value.
(Family side) "No need to involve the executor."Only the executor or administrator can authorize a cleanout and the handling of the contents. Anyone who says otherwise is a risk.
